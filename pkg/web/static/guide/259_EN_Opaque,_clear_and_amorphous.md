# Opaque, clear and amorphous

![Glass](item:glass)

The [microscope](238) lights the slide from below: the mirror sends light up **through** it. That is perfect for a drop of pond water, and it decides what you can see of the [lifeless things](258) you put on the stage.

## Clear things: almost invisible

Quartz, salt, ice and glass let nearly all the light through, so you see them by their **edges**, where the light bends. A grain of quartz in sand is little more than a dark outline. A bubble in ice has a thick dark ring and a bright middle: light bends away at its edge.

With [dark-field](247), clear things show best of all: only their edges shine, on black.

## Coloured things: the colour is inside

Ruby, sapphire, cobalt glass and stained glass are coloured all through. In glass the colour is metal **dissolved** in it (cobalt for blue), not grains of paint: even at 1000× there are no particles to find. Copper sulphate, sulfur and fluorite let their colour through in the same way, deeper where the crystal is thicker.

Gold leaf is the surprise: gold beaten to about a tenth of a micrometre lets light through, and it comes out **green**.

## Opaque things: black

A metal, most ores, a silicon wafer, the silver of a mirror, the copper of a track: no light gets through them at all. By light from below they are **black silhouettes**. A light bulb's filament is a black coil; a mirror is black except where a scratch or a pinhole lets a point of light through.

At 40× the objective stands far from the slide, so the light of the room still falls on the top of what is there, and you see its surface: the brass of pyrite, the grains of an etched metal, the silver fingers of a solar cell. At 100× the lens comes closer and that light fades. At 400× and 1000× the lens almost touches the slide: the surface is dark.

Real metallurgists, geologists and chip makers use a microscope that lights the specimen **through the objective itself**, from above: reflected light. This microscope does not have one.

## Amorphous: nothing at all

Put `glass` on the stage and look at every power: a bubble, some dust, a scratch, and **nothing else**. That is the lesson. Glass is **amorphous**: its atoms are in disorder, with no grains, no crystals and no cells. Obsidian is glass made by a volcano: it shows only a few tiny crystals (microlites) that began to grow as the lava cooled, lined up with its flow.

## Why stains do nothing to them

[Stains](246) work on living stuff: iodine finds starch, methylene blue holds on to nuclei. A mineral, a metal or glass has neither, so the dye sits on top and is washed off. Salts are different: the **water** in the dye dissolves them, and their corners round off.

Geologists have their own tricks: **alizarin red** turns calcite pink-red, and **polarised light** (two filters, one under the slide and one above, crossed) makes each crystal glow in its own colour. Glass stays black between crossed filters, being amorphous. This microscope has no polarisers.

## Science and game

**True:**

- Transmitted light, edges of clear things, black opaque things, green gold leaf, dissolved metals colouring glass, alizarin red and polarised light are all as described.
- A low-power objective really does stand far from the slide (around 18 mm for a common 4×), and a 40× one a fraction of a millimetre.

**Simplified in the game:**

- How much room light reaches the slide is the same everywhere; really it depends on the lamp above the bench.
