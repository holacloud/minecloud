# Filters and dark-field

![Dark-field stop](item:darkfield_stop)

Stains change the **slide**. These two change the **light**. Neither is used up, and neither touches the slide: they are yours alone, and whoever is looking at the same microscope sees it their own way. They are not saved: reload and they are off.

## Colour filters

Carry a coloured glass and it can go in the light path. Press **Filter** in [the panel](238) and pick from what you carry: ![](item:cobalt_glass) `cobalt_glass`, or any of the [16 stained glasses](229), block or pane.

A filter lets its own colour through and holds back the rest, so the whole field takes that colour. It adds to what the light already has: a candle's light is orange, and through a blue filter it turns out darker and less blue than the sun through the same glass.

**The trick:** a filter can make a stain stand out. A dye looks dark when the filter passes what the dye absorbs.

| On the slide | Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| [Methylene blue](246) | orange or red glass | blue dye absorbs orange: nuclei look nearly black |
| [Iodine](246) | blue glass | brown dye absorbs blue: it looks darker |
| Either | the dye's own colour | the dye passes that light, and it fades |

With a filter in place the panel tells you which way it went for the stain on your slide.

## Dark-field

| | In-game name | Id | Recipe (C key) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ![](item:darkfield_stop) | Dark-field Stop | `darkfield_stop` | 1 `glass_slide` + 1 `coal_ore` |

A black disc on clear glass, under the slide. It blocks the light going straight up, and only what comes in at a slant gets past. That light misses the lens, unless something on the slide scatters it. So the background goes **black**, and whatever is on the slide **shines at its edges**, unstained and alive.

Carry one and press **Dark-field** in the panel. It works with every organism and with anything else on the slide, which means every speck of dirt shines too.

**It needs strong light.** Nearly all the light is thrown away, so what is enough for a normal view is not enough here:

| Light beside it | Dark-field |
|---|---|
| [Light bulb](187) | very dim |
| [Candle](228), low sun at dawn or dusk | dim to faint |
| Table lamp, lantern, [electric lamp](204), full sun | bright |

The panel says which one you have. Try the smallest things as well: at 100× you cannot make out a [soil bacterium](244), but on black it shows as a point of light.

## Science and game

**True:**

- A dark-field stop really is an opaque disc in the light path, and a simple one can be home-made. It blocks the central rays and leaves only the slanting ones.
- Only light scattered by the specimen reaches the objective. This wastes so much light that it needs a strong lamp.
- Slides have to be very clean, as dust shines against the black.
- It does not make the microscope sharper: it shows things by contrast. Doctors have used it for spiral bacteria about 0.1 µm wide, too thin and too hard to stain for a normal view. You can tell that they are there and how they move, not what they look like in detail.
- Filters do raise contrast: a filter of the opposite colour makes a coloured thing look darker. Green is the most common.
- Microscopists use a blue filter against the yellow-orange cast of a dim filament lamp. In the game a pale blue glass does the same with a flame: the field is less orange, and darker.

**Simplified in the game:**

- Here a filter multiplies only three numbers, red, green and blue. A real one works on every wavelength, so two glasses that look alike can behave differently.
- Your stop works at every power, without adjusting it. A real simple stop must suit the objective, and does well up to about a 40× objective; beyond that you need a special condenser with oil.
- Stained glass from a window is not a filter from an optics catalogue. Real ones are made to pass a known colour.
- Brightness here is one number that follows the light beside the microscope. In a real one, you also line up the condenser.
- A stain and dark-field can be used together, but nobody really gains from that: real dark-field is for what has not been stained.

> **Try it for real:** a coin stuck in the middle of a clear glass disc under the condenser of a school microscope gives dark-field at low power. Pond water looks like a night sky.
