# Maya palace

![Red Lime Stucco](item:red_stucco)

Deep in the jungle, a paved plaza with two carved stones leads to a **great stair**, 21 blocks wide, that climbs a platform of red and white. On top stand long buildings painted red, with stepped roofs, friezes of zigzag patterns and pierced crests along their tops. Nearby rise a **stepped pyramid**, a **ball court** and a round well of water. It is the **Maya palace**, and its science is the Maya sky and their numbers.

## What inspired it

It is inspired by the royal palaces of the **Classic Maya** (about the years 250 to 900), above all **Palenque** and **Tikal**, in today's Mexico and Guatemala. It is not a copy of either. The Maya built cities of stone in the rainforest, wrote in glyphs, and were among the best astronomers of the ancient world. What it borrows from them:

- Walls of ![](item:limestone) **limestone** covered in **lime stucco** and painted red and white, with a touch of jade green.
- The **corbel vault**: the Maya did not use the round arch. Each row of stones juts out a little over the one below, from both walls, until the two sides almost meet and a capstone closes the top. That is why the rooms are long and narrow.
- **Roof combs**: tall pierced crests on top of the grand buildings, to make them look higher.
- A city **not symmetric**, as Maya cities were not: the palace is the ordered part, and the pyramid, the ball court and the well stand where the land puts them.

## A walk through it

**The plaza and the sacbe.** At the entrance, two **stelae** (tall carved stones) with their round altars and a candle on each. From the plaza a white road, a *sacbe* ("white road" in Maya), leads to the foot of the pyramid and to the **cenote**.

**The great platform**, in two stepped tiers, with the great stair in front between balustrades that end in serpent heads, braziers at its corners, and a smaller stair on the side towards the pyramid. Every building on it is a long *range* of rooms under a corbel vault. The Maya had no wooden doors in their palaces: they hung **cloth in the doorways**, and here some curtains are drawn back and some let down. Things lie where people left them: plates of a meal half eaten, books open, a game half played.

- **The South Gallery**: an open portico with stone benches for those who wait, and a mask of the sun god over the passage. Behind it, the **Audience Gallery**, where the lords received on raised benches under banners, with a **guard room** at one end and the **tribute store** at the other, full of what the towns sent the king: cloth, feathers, salt, honey, maize, obsidian.
- **The Main Court**, with a round altar in the middle and **five jade stones** round it (the four corners of the world and its centre), stelae, braziers and a **ceiba**, the sacred tree that holds up the middle of the world. On its west side, the **scribes' room** (books on the shelves, desks, a mat), the **workshop** of carvers and weavers and the servants' room. On its east side, the **kitchen** (ovens, a hearth of three stones, a grinding stone, barrels of maize), the **cacao and honey store** and the **treasury**, the only room with a door.
- **The Throne House**: the ![](item:jade_throne) jade throne on a white dais with jade at its corners, banners of red, yellow and blue behind it and nobles' benches on both sides; a great mask looks out over the court. Behind it, the **banquet hall**, with two long tables and barrels of *balché*, a drink of honey.
- **The Tower Court**: the four-storey **observatory tower**, a ![](item:sundial) sundial, a ceiba and a stela, with the **Calendar Hall** along its back. Beside it, the **Royal Residence**: the king's room, the largest, with a letter half written on his desk; smaller bedrooms for the queen and the princes, with mats and woven cloth on the walls; the king's dressing room; a sitting room with a board game left half played; the princes' schoolroom; and three small **washrooms**, each with a seat and a basin of water. Then the **temazcal** (a domed steam bath, where water poured on glowing hot stones turns to steam) and a garden with a pool of still water.

**The Jaguar Temple**, a pyramid of **seven tiers** with a stair up its front and a temple with its own roof comb at the top, braziers on its terrace and offerings inside.

**The ball court**, behind the pyramid: two long buildings face each other across an alley, each with a stone **ring** high on its wall. Players struck a heavy rubber ball with their hips, never their hands, and putting it through the ring was very hard.

## The science corner: numbers, calendars and the sky

### Counting in twenties

The **Calendar Hall** has a **counting board** in its floor, with five numbers written the Maya way, in black dots and bars and a yellow shell. The Maya counted **in twenties**, with the fingers of their hands *and* feet, and they needed only three signs:

| Sign | Value |
|---|---|
| a **dot** | 1 |
| a **bar** | 5 |
| a **shell** | 0 |

On the board: **0** (a shell), **3** (three dots), **5** (a bar), **12** (two bars and two dots) and **19** (three bars and four dots, the biggest number one place can hold).

For bigger numbers they **stacked places**, as we line up our digits: the bottom place counts ones, the one above counts **twenties**. On the ground floor of the tower, **twenty** is written as a dot above a shell: one twenty and no units, just as our 10 is a one and a zero. So **45** would be two dots (two twenties = 40) over a bar (5).

### The zero

The shell is **zero**. The Maya invented it on their own, more than 1,500 years ago, without contact with India or Babylon, which found their own zeros too. Without a zero you cannot write the difference between 2 and 20 (or 40) with the same signs.

### Two calendars at once

On the walls of the hall, the **20 day signs** go round the room in their order: Imix, Ik', Ak'bal, K'an... each a glyph of coloured wool. The Maya used two calendars at the same time:

- The **Tzolk'in**, of **260 days**: the 20 day signs combined with the numbers 1 to 13 (13 × 20 = 260).
- The **Haab'**, the year of the Sun, of **365 days**: 18 months of 20 days (360) and 5 more days, thought unlucky.

Like two gears of different sizes, the two calendars only come back to the same pair of days every **52 years**: the *Calendar Round*.

### Venus and the Sun

The tower is the observatory, one storey for each lesson:

- **Ground floor:** the number twenty written in the floor.
- **First floor: Venus.** Venus comes back to the same place in the sky every **584 days**. The Maya wrote it down in their books, like the **Dresden Codex**, one of only four Maya books that survive. Five of those Venus cycles (5 × 584 = 2,920 days) make exactly **eight** years of 365 days.
- **Second floor: the Sun.** The Sun does not always rise in the same place: further north in summer, further south in winter. The astronomer's bed is here, because they watched all night.
- **Third floor: the equinoxes.** Twice a year, around 20 March and 22 September, the Sun rises **due east** and sets **due west**. The Maya lined up windows and doors with it to know the date. A yellow line on the floor joins the two windows opposite each other.
- **The roof:** here they watched the night sky with no telescope, only their eyes and a lot of patience.

> **Try it:** at the equinox at the pyramid of **Chichén Itzá**, in Mexico, the afternoon Sun draws triangles of light down the edge of the great stair, and they look like a serpent sliding down to the stone head at its foot.

### More signs to read

Around the palace, more signs explain the everyday science of the Maya:

- **Cenote:** a natural well. The roof of a limestone cave fell in and showed the water underneath.
- **Scribes:** they painted their books on paper made of fig-tree bark coated with lime.
- **Nixtamal:** maize was cooked with lime ([quicklime](183) in water). That makes it more nourishing and easier to grind.
- **Cacao and honey:** cacao was drunk frothy, and its beans were money. The honey came from stingless bees.
- **Latrine:** the palace of Palenque had stone seats over a channel of running water that carried the dirt away.
- **The ball:** it was rubber. They mixed the latex of a tree with the juice of a vine to make it bounce, some 3,000 years before rubber factories existed.

## What the chests hold

- **Kitchen, stores and banquet hall:** maize (wheat), seeds, bread, fish, mushrooms, honey, salt, ![](item:quicklime) quicklime and buckets of water.
- **Tribute store and treasury:** wool of many colours, feathers, salt, honeycomb, obsidian, amber, pyrite, gold leaf and ore, a mirror.
- **Scribes' room, tower and Calendar Hall:** star charts, red pigment, white stucco, feathers, candles, a sundial, blueprints and a measuring tape.
- **Workshop:** obsidian, basalt, granite, wool, feathers, planks and jungle saplings.
- **Temple:** offerings of obsidian, amber, feathers, honeycomb, candles and a little gold leaf.
- **Residence and temazcal:** wool and feathers, amber, bread, red pigment, a star chart, buckets and cold water.

## Curiosities

- **Obsidian**, a volcanic glass, was the Maya's steel: its edges are sharper than a modern razor blade.
- The cenotes of Yucatán form a **ring** round the buried crater of **Chicxulub**, left by the asteroid that ended the age of the dinosaurs 66 million years ago (see [Extinct animals](261)).
- Palenque's palace really has a **four-storey tower**, unusual in Maya buildings; many think it was used to watch the sky.
- The word **chocolate** comes from the languages of Mexico, and so does the word for cacao. Cacao beans were still used as coins in the markets of Mexico when the Spanish arrived.
- Millions of Maya people live in Mexico and Central America today, and many still speak Maya languages.
