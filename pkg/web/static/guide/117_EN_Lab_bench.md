# Lab bench

![Lab bench](item:lab_bench)

A wooden cabinet with a white tiled worktop, a burner and a green stain from some experiment. This is where
[element blocks](60) turn into something useful.

| | In-game name | Id |
|---|---|---|
| ![](item:lab_bench) | Lab Bench | `lab_bench` |

## How to get it

| Recipe (C key) | Ingredients |
|---|---|
| 1 `lab_bench` | 4 `planks` + 2 `glass` + 1 `iron_ore` |

## How to use it

- Place it like any block. **Right click** it (whatever you hold) to open the lab.
- The lab has three parts:
  - **The periodic table**: the 36 elements in their place, with the atomic number at the top and, at the bottom
    in yellow, how many blocks you carry. The ones you lack are dimmed. Click to drop an atom into the flask,
    right click to take it out. Under the table you see the details of the last element clicked, with a **📖**
    button that opens its page in this guide.
  - **The flask**: shows the mix as an equation, for example `2 H + O → H₂O (Water)`, what it makes and what you
    are missing. The **React** button runs the reaction; **Empty flask** empties it.
  - **The reaction list**: 30 in all. At first only 4 are known; the rest show as **???** until you mix the right
    atoms in the flask and **discover** them (they are remembered for good). Click a known one to fill the flask;
    its **📖** button opens the product's page. The ones you can make right now are green. The header shows how
    many you have discovered.
- Escape, the **Close** button or a click outside the panel closes it. A 📖 button also closes it and opens the
  guide; closing the book takes you back to the game.

## Good to know

- Breaking it: **0.8 s** by hand (it is wooden: an [axe](70) breaks it faster). It drops the bench itself.
- Since right click opens it, **you cannot place blocks against its faces**: place them first, or put the bench
  down last.
- Each player uses their own inventory: the bench is shared, the elements are not.

## Trivia

- The gas burners of school labs are called **Bunsen burners**, after the German chemist Robert Bunsen, who made
  them popular in 1855.
- Lab worktops are made of materials that resist acids, such as ceramic or special resins.
