# Fossils

![Fossil rock](item:fossil_rock)

Far down, inside the stone, there are rocks with a drawing on them: a coiled shell, a footprint, a skull. They are **fossils**: what is left of animals that lived a very long time ago. Break the rock and you take the fossil with you. This page says what they look like, where they are and how to search for them. [Amber](263) has a page of its own.

## Where they are

Look at your height, the **y**. Water is at y = 5 and the bottom of the world at y = −14. A **chunk** is a piece of the world 16 × 16 blocks across ([How the world is born](99)).

| | Where | Height | How many |
|---|---|---|---|
| Common fossil | in the stone, under any biome | from y = −11 to y = 2 | two or three pockets in nearly every chunk, of 1 to 3 blocks each |
| Rare fossil | in the stone, under any biome | from y = −11 to y = −3 | one block in 4 chunks out of 5 |
| Skeleton | in the stone, under any biome | from y = −11 to y = −4 | its three blocks in 1 chunk out of 14 |
| Recent bones | under the sand of a **beach**, and now and then under the grass of a **plain** | 1 to 3 blocks under the ground | on a beach, 1 column in 110; on a plain, 1 in 1500 |

- The rarer a fossil is, the **deeper** it starts. Between y = −11 and y = −4 anything may turn up.
- In the stone, a fossil only takes the place of plain stone. It never replaces an ore, and there is none in the bedrock or in the block above it.
- A **beach** is the sand by the water in plains and forests. The sand of the desert does not count, and neither does the bottom of a lake.

A piece of the world you explored before stays as it was, with no fossils. They only appear in land **nobody has visited yet**.

## How to search for them

- **Common and rare fossils.** Nothing shows from outside: you have to dig. Go down to about y = −6 and open a long, straight tunnel. Watch the walls, the floor and the ceiling as you go. When a shell shows, keep digging around it: a pocket has up to three blocks that touch.
- **Skeletons.** Their three blocks are always **in a straight line and at the same height**, with the spine in the middle. The skull is 2 or 3 blocks from the spine, and the tail another 2 or 3 on the opposite side. All three are always in the same chunk. When you find one piece, dig at its own height towards the four sides, up to 6 blocks: the other two are there.
- **Recent bones.** Go to a beach with a [shovel](69) and take the sand away, up to three blocks deep. They are the easiest to find.

## What you take with you

- Breaking the block does not give you the rock, but the **fossil of the animal** that was inside.
- Which animal it is depends on the exact place of the block. It is the same for every player, but nobody knows until it is broken.
- The three blocks of a skeleton belong to **one animal**: they give you its skull, its spine and its tail. A big animal needs all three pieces.
- Fossil rock is a little harder than stone. A [pickaxe](68) makes it quicker; for recent bones, a shovel.
- A [robot](193) that digs gets the same as you from the same block.
- If the game has no animal yet for that kind of fossil, the rock gives only `cobblestone` and recent bones give `dirt`.

## How a real fossil is made

Hardly any animal ends up as a fossil. Usually others eat it, or it rots away. For something to be left, all of this has to happen:

1. **It is buried soon**, in the mud at the bottom of a lake or of the sea, in sand or in ash. Nothing can eat it there, and hardly any air reaches it.
2. **Layers pile up on top** for thousands of years. Under so much weight, the mud slowly turns into stone.
3. **Water brings minerals.** It seeps between the grains and leaves minerals in the gaps of the bone or the shell, until a copy in stone is left.

That is why nearly all fossils are of **hard parts**: shells, bones and teeth. And it is why they take so long: scientists only call something a fossil when it is more than about 10,000 years old, and most are millions of years old.

- **The oldest lies deepest.** Layers of rock pile up one on another, so the lower ones are the older ones. Something like that happens here: the rare ones and the skeletons lie deeper.
- **A footprint is a fossil too.** If an animal steps on soft mud and the mud turns into stone, the footprint stays. That is how we know how dinosaurs walked.
- **The commonest ones lived in the sea.** Trilobites and ammonites had hard shells, there were a great many of them, and they sank into the mud of the bottom. Trilobites died out about 252 million years ago, and ammonites 66 million years ago, together with the dinosaurs.

## Why the dodo is not in the stone

The dodo was a big bird that could not fly and lived on the island of Mauritius. It died out about 350 years ago, less than a hundred years after the first ships arrived. That is **a very short time**: its bones have had no time to turn into stone. They are still bone.

That is why in the game it is not in the rock, but near the surface. In 1865 a great many dodo bones were found in a swamp by the coast of the island.

> **A girl who looked for fossils.** Mary Anning was 12 when she and her brother dug the skeleton of an ichthyosaur, a reptile of the sea, out of a cliff in England. That was around 1811. Years later, in 1823, she found the first complete skeleton of a **plesiosaur**. If you ever find a real fossil, take a photo and show it to a museum.

A fossil comes back to life in the [revival machine](264): it needs power, a signal and, for a sea animal, water.
